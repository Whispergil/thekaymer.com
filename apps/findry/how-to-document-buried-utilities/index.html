How to Document Buried Utilities

Record it before you backfill, so you can find it later.

Once a trench is closed, the pipe, conduit, or cable inside it is out of sight. A few minutes of documentation before backfill can save hours of searching, guesswork, and risky digging later. This guide covers what to capture: photos, location, depth, material and size, and notes, and how to save it so the work can be found again. It applies to water and sewer lines, storm drains, irrigation, gas, electrical conduit, and telecom.

Call 811 before you dig. In the United States, call 811 or submit a request online a few working days before any excavation so existing utilities can be marked. Your own records do not replace a locate request.

Step by step

How to document buried utilities in 7 steps

Do this while the trench is still open. Once it is backfilled, you cannot go back for the photo you missed.

Step 1: Call 811 before you dig

Before any excavation, call 811 or submit a request online a few working days ahead so existing buried utilities can be marked. Write the locate ticket number down. You will add it to your record later. Local rules vary, so follow the requirements in your state.

Step 2: Photograph the work before backfill

Once the pipe, conduit, or cable is in place and before any backfill goes in, take photos. Get a wide shot that shows the trench with a building, curb, or other fixed feature in view, then close-ups of fittings, valves, tees, bends, and connections. Lay a tape measure in the trench for scale and shoot a photo that shows the depth.

Step 3: Record the location

Tie the location to things that will still be there later, like building corners, curbs, hydrants, manholes, valve boxes, or property pins. Two measurements from two fixed points are easier to re-find than one. If you capture GPS coordinates with your phone or a GPS device, write them down too, but keep the tape measurements: phone GPS can be off by several feet.

Step 4: Measure and write down the depth

Measure from finished grade to the top of the pipe, and note where along the run you measured. Depth often changes, so take a reading at each end, at bends, and at any fittings or valves.

Step 5: Note the material and size

Record what was installed: the utility type (water, sewer, storm, gas, electric, telecom), the pipe or conduit material (for example PVC, HDPE, or ductile iron), the size, and the length of the run. Add any warning tape, tracer wire, or markers you put in the trench.

Step 6: Add notes

Write down the date, the crew or contractor, the locate ticket number, the project address, and anything the next person should know, like a change from the plan, a nearby line, or a shallow spot.

Step 7: Save the record where you can find it later

A record only helps if you can find it again. Keep photos and notes together, organized by project, instead of spread across a notebook, a text thread, and a camera roll. Make sure the record can be searched by address, project, or utility type, and back it up.

Checklist

Buried utility record checklist

  • Photos before backfill: wide shot with a fixed feature, close-ups of fittings, and a depth photo with a tape
  • Location: measurements from two fixed reference points, plus GPS coordinates if you have them
  • Depth to the top of the pipe, with where each reading was taken
  • Utility type, material, size, and length of run
  • Valves, cleanouts, tees, bends, tracer wire, and warning tape
  • Date, crew or contractor, project address, and 811 locate ticket number
  • Notes on changes from the plan, nearby lines, or shallow spots
  • Saved by project, searchable, and backed up

FAQ

Documenting buried utilities FAQ

Why document buried utilities?

After backfill, a pipe or conduit is out of sight. A good record shows the next crew, the owner, or you, what is down there and where, so the work can be found for repairs, tie-ins, or new digging without guessing.

What should a buried utility record include?

At minimum: photos before backfill, the location tied to fixed reference points, the depth, the utility type, the material and size, the date, who did the work, and the locate ticket number. GPS coordinates and notes about warnings or changes make it even more useful.

Can Findry find or detect underground pipes?

No. Findry does not detect or locate underground utilities. It is a documentation app: it stores the photos, depths, measurements, reference points, and notes you save about the work you install or expose, so you can find that work later. It does not replace 811 or professional locating.

Do I still need to call 811 if I have records?

Yes. Always call 811 before you dig. Your own records help you find your own work, but other utilities may be in the ground, and the law in most places requires a locate request before excavation.

Can I add GPS coordinates to a Findry record?

Yes. If you capture coordinates with your phone or a separate GPS device, you can type them into the record notes. Keep tape measurements to fixed reference points as well, because they are often more accurate than phone GPS.
